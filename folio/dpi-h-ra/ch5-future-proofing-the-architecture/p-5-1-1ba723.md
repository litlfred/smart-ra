It is also relevant that not all emerging technologies draw on data held in the shared infrastructure. Some consumer-facing applications operate independently of health-system data, using information entered by individuals or collected through personal devices to offer self-management support or lifestyle advice, and these can deliver value without accessing shared records or interoperating with wider health information systems. However, because their benefits depend on **individual activation**, personal device ownership, reliable and affordable connectivity, fluency in supported languages, functional ability, general and digital literacy, and often a stable digital identity, reliance on standalone consumer-facing tools can exclude underserved populations and widen health inequities. Where health systems endorse, integrate, or rely on patient-facing digital tools, countries should assess the risk of digital exclusion and require accessibility across functional abilities, affordability, multilingual and cross-language support (without assuming the person and health worker share a language), support for varying patient and health worker digital literacy, and **assisted and non-digital pathways of equivalent standing** for any function on which care access, rights, or entitlements depend. The architecture described in this chapter concerns those technologies that depend on, or contribute to, the shared health data foundation — where value accrues to every person as a reference point regardless of personal device ownership — and it is for these that the relationship set out below is decisive.
