Equity and safety warrant particular attention. A model trained on data that is poorly governed or unrepresentative of the population it serves can reproduce and magnify existing inequities, and because it does so consistently and at scale, the effect can be harder to notice than an individual error. Moreover, systems that appear equitable in aggregate or across single demographic categories may still perform poorly for people experiencing overlapping disadvantages — across combinations of poverty, disability, functional ability, language, literacy, geography, migration status, age, gender, and digital exclusion — or fail populations that are missing from formal datasets altogether or whose clinical history was captured across a language barrier without adequate contextual meaning. Where lawful, proportionate, and ethically justified, countries should require intersectional validation and ongoing distributional monitoring of AI-enabled tools, account for missing populations and cultural and linguistic fit without unnecessary collection of sensitive data, distinguish measurable health differences from avoidable and unjust health inequities, and preserve human clinical oversight and assisted or non-digital fallback pathways. Attention to the representativeness, contextual fidelity, and quality of the underlying data is therefore not a separate ethical exercise but part of the same data-foundation discipline on which clinical safety and performance depend.
