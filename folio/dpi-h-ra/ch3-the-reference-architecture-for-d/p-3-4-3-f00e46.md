**Operationalising** **equity and distributional impact assessment in governance.** Representation within governance bodies is necessary but not sufficient; governance must also assess and act on the actual distributional effects of architectural, standards, and policy decisions. Design considerations include: formal representation of civil society, disability and patient organisations, and community health advocates with a demonstrated constituency in underserved populations; geographic representation across subnational regions, particularly where health system capacity and connectivity vary; gender, disability, and linguistic inclusion in the composition of governance bodies; mechanisms for soliciting and incorporating feedback and community-defined priorities from populations that lack organised representation; and mandatory **equity-impact assessments** for major architectural, procurement, and governance decisions. Such assessments should examine who gains access and under what preconditions, who bears data-entry, administrative, and compliance burdens (across frontline health workers, under-resourced facilities, and patients), who risks exclusion when digital channels or identity requirements are introduced, whether assisted and non-digital routes of equivalent standing are maintained, and whether benefits and risks are distributed fairly across regions and population groups. Where lawful, proportionate, and ethically justified, governance should require evaluation across combinations of overlapping characteristics and contexts — such as poverty, disability, functional ability, language, literacy, geography, migration status, age, gender, and digital exclusion — rather than relying solely on single-group averages, while requiring explicit attention to populations missing from formal datasets, missing contextual meaning (including where patient and clinician do not share a fluent language), and cultural and linguistic fit without unnecessary collection of sensitive personal data.
