The outcomes these goals describe are not automatic. In every country, a proportion of the population cannot or chooses not to use self-service digital channels, and routine internet or device use is distinct from both basic digital skills and the health literacy needed to find, understand, and appraise health information. If the architecture's goals were achievable only through personal digital channels, the goals themselves would encode exclusion; accordingly, every function on which a person's access to care, rights, or health entitlements depends should maintain a non-digital or assisted route of equivalent standing as an operational readiness condition for deployment rather than a future aspiration. The extent to which these goals are realised depends on the quality of implementation and the context in which it takes place; evidence to date shows that benefits anticipated in design are not always borne out in practice. For this reason, the implementation of DPI-H should be evaluated as it proceeds — to evidence the benefits actually delivered, assess distributional effects across populations experiencing overlapping disadvantages, identify and mitigate exclusion risks, and share learning across programmes and countries. Together, these goals point towards a health system that has moved beyond digitisation as an end in itself — toward a system that is more equitable in who it reaches, more efficient in how it operates, more responsive to the people who deliver care, and more resilient in the face of the challenges that are increasingly certain to come.
