Building on this understanding, the G20[^7] described DPI as a set of shared digital systems that should be secure and interoperable, and can be built on open standards and specifications to deliver and provide equitable access to public and/or private services at societal scale and are governed by applicable legal frameworks and enabling rules to drive development, inclusion, innovation, trust, and competition, while respecting human rights and fundamental freedoms. Taken together, these definitions establish foundational characteristics of DPI: that it is shared socio-technical infrastructure operating at societal scale, and that its value derives not from any single system or agency asset, but from its open, interoperable, and publicly governed nature upon which public, private, and civil-society actors can build to create public value. The G20 itself recognises that DPI is an evolving concept, not necessarily limited to systems with these characteristics, and one that may be tailored to specific country contexts and referred to using different terminologies.<sup>7</sup> Complementing this consensus, the [Universal DPI Safeguards Framework](https://www.dpi-safeguards.org/framework) emphasises that DPI must be accompanied by foundational and operational safeguards — including inclusive governance, accountability, data protection, and the preservation of assisted and non-digital access routes — so that digitalisation advances inclusion and human rights rather than creating new barriers to essential services. This provides the basis for articulating a health-specific understanding of DPI, adapted to the needs of the health sector while remaining consistent with the broader cross-sectoral consensus. It is from this understanding that health-specific digital public infrastructure is derived.
