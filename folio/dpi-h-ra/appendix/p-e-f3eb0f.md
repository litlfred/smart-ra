**What the health system needs to be able to do:** the system needs to know who all of its health and care workers are and what each is qualified and authorised to do — covering statutory licensed practitioners overseen by health practitioner regulators as well as non-regulated cadres, community health workers, multiskilled personnel, provisional registrants, students and trainees, and volunteers authorised through national programme or employer mechanisms where formal licensure or foundational national identity is absent. It must hold an authoritative reference record of workforce identity, occupational and role classification, educational qualifications, and professional registration or authorisation status that clinical, administrative, financing, and regulatory systems can rely on, while explicitly separating verified registration and qualifications from current clinical competence, task-specific authorisation, facility-level clinical privileging, fitness to practise, and authorised scope of practice (which depend on ongoing clinical governance, credential verification, supervision, competence assessment, and regulatory standing). To align with the WHO National Health Workforce Accounts (NHWA) and the health labour market framework, it needs to distinguish among workers who are *licensed to practise*, *professionally active*, and *practising*, and enable national and subnational planners and policymakers to track active workforce capacity, skill mix, and subnational distribution so that imbalances in the health labour market can be identified and minimised. Rather than inferring staffing adequacy from worker location and facility affiliation alone, the system needs analytical and planning capabilities that combine workforce deployment, contract/full-time equivalent (FTE) capacity, and scope of practice with catchment population need, service demand, workload, facility readiness, and infrastructure data, alongside external statistical and administrative sources of varying digital maturity (such as population censuses, labour force surveys, and national pension or social-security schemes). Finally, it needs to pay workers reliably and uphold labour rights (including salary, leave, pension, accreditation, and continuing professional development) by connecting verified workforce identity and active deployment records with operational HR, contract, attendance, public financial management, payroll, and learning systems — keeping detailed HR administrative actions, extended leaves, pay calculations, and course-level learning records inside those operational systems while enforcing strict compliance with data confidentiality and privacy laws, purpose-limited dataset sharing, worker transparency, and avenues for correction and appeal.
