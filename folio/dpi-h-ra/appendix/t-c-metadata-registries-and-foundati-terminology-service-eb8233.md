| **GOVERNANCE** | Governance of the Terminology Service follows the cross-cutting governance framework in Section 3.4 of the guidance document. Component-specific considerations include:<br>(1) Institutional ownership, decision rights, and approval authority. Stewardship should be vested in a national health authority or a delegated standards body with a legal or institutional mandate spanning all health programmes and domains, not in a single vendor, project, or programme. The national TS is shared infrastructure and should be governed in the public interest. Where multiple professional bodies, regulatory agencies, or health programmes are involved, the governance framework should explicitly define decision rights, domain-authority roles, and approval responsibilities by artefact type: (a) **national extensions** of international code systems (such as SNOMED CT national extensions or national ICD modifications) are approved by the designated national terminology authority or National Release Centre in consultation with the relevant clinical specialty or classification domain authority; (b) **national language translations and designations** are approved jointly by the national terminology authority, qualified clinical and linguistic reviewers for the target language, and the relevant domain authority in accordance with upstream licensing and translation rules; (c) **value sets** are approved for clinical or programmatic content by the mandated domain authority or programme owner (or a joint cross-programme review panel where a value set is shared across multiple programmes) and for semantic and technical conformance by the terminology authority; and (d) **concept maps and cross-terminology mappings** are approved jointly by the source and target domain authorities and the national terminology authority (including classification and clinical coding specialists where statistical classifications are mapped) after verifying equivalence scope and use-case fitness. Final **publication authority** is separated from authoring and domain endorsement and vested in the national TS steward once all clinical, semantic, licensing, and technical gates are satisfied.<br>(2) Multi-disciplinary governance. Terminology governance requires active participation of clinical and public health domain experts, not only informaticians, to ensure that national code systems, extensions, translations, value sets, and mappings reflect local clinical practice, epidemiology, and reporting needs. Governance committees should include clinicians, public health professionals, pharmacists, and community health specialists alongside technical terminology experts and classification and clinical coding specialists, operating under defined reviewer qualifications, conflict-of-interest disclosure and recusal rules, and quorum requirements so that both direct clinical concept capture and statistical classification use are properly governed.<br>(3) Content lifecycle management, public review, conflict resolution, appeals, and emergency procedures. A clear content lifecycle — draft, review, approved, published, deprecated, retired — with explicit policies on backwards compatibility and version pinning is essential. Countries should define the minimum lead time for deprecating a code that is in active use in clinical systems, and the mechanism through which affected systems are notified. The lifecycle framework should also specify: (a) proportionate **public and stakeholder review** for high-impact national code systems, extensions, translations, value sets, and mappings, with published responses and explanations of disposition; (b) a tiered **conflict-resolution mechanism** when professional bodies or health programmes disagree on concept definitions, value-set membership, or mapping equivalence, escalating from domain working groups to the multi-disciplinary terminology governance committee and, ultimately, the mandated national standards authority; (c) a documented **appeals procedure** enabling programmes, professional bodies, or implementers to contest terminology or mapping decisions; and (d) **emergency change procedures** (for example, for novel pathogens, public health emergencies, urgent pharmacovigilance alerts, or critical coding defects) that permit rapid provisional publication under designated emergency authority followed by mandatory retrospective multi-disciplinary review and ratification within a defined timeframe.<br>(4) Licensing obligations. Licensing of proprietary terminologies, notably SNOMED CT, should be planned as an institutional commitment including national member fees where applicable. The TS governance framework should define how licence compliance is monitored, how access credentials are managed for individual consuming systems, and what happens when a consumer's licence lapses.<br>(5) International coordination. The Terminology Service governance framework should establish processes for monitoring upstream releases from international standards development organisations, for reviewing and testing new releases before national adoption, for managing the transition period during which multiple versions may be in active use, and for submitting local change requests and feedback to international bodies. For ICD-based statistical reporting, this coordination should identify the applicable ICD revision, release policy, coding rules and transition arrangements needed to meet the Member State's reporting obligations. Proposed changes to WHO content should be routed through WHO's maintenance process. National adoption of a new release should be tested and documented, preserving the reproducibility of earlier reports. An adopted ICD revision and an annual content or API release are distinct: the availability of a new upstream release does not make it immediately mandatory prior to national evaluation and adoption, nor does it alter a Member State's statistical reporting revision without a governed national transition.<br>(6) Relationship with Semantic Governance Infrastructure. The TS is a component of the broader semantic governance infrastructure that also includes the LIMR. Governance of the two components should be coordinated rather than managed in isolation, particularly regarding change management that crosses the boundary between terminology content and structural model definitions. |
