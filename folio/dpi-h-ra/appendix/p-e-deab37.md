**Goal:** mobilise, pool, and allocate health funds efficiently and equitably, purchase covered services transparently, pay providers and health workers reliably, and protect people from financial hardship when they seek care.

**What the health system needs to be able to do:** the system needs to define and publish what health services and products are covered under each scheme, enrol eligible individuals and households, and enable patients and providers to understand entitlements clearly — pairing machine-readable definitions with plain-language, multilingual, accessible explanations and assisted-access support. It needs to empanel and contract qualified facilities and health workers, establish transparent tariffs, fee schedules, and payment methods, verify eligibility and prior authorisation where required, and adjudicate claims against clinical coding rules and verifiable evidence of service delivery. And it needs to execute lawful, timely payments to facilities, suppliers, and health workers through public financial management, payroll, and digital payment systems while safeguarding public funds through internal controls, audit, anomaly detection, investigation, appeal mechanisms, and independent oversight.

**How to read the mapping:** the method is illustrated in the worked example in Section 2.4.2, and the spreadsheet that follows traces each financing capability from the goal through stakeholder value, business capability, and application service to the shared DPI-H reference components, foundational-DPI dependencies, and operational financing systems that together realise it. Reading the mapping requires keeping six architectural boundaries explicit:

- **Shared DPI-H reference components versus operational financing systems.** Within the scope of DPI-H, the principal domain-specific shared reference component is the Benefits Package Registry, which publishes authoritative benefit package definitions, coverage parameters, and testable machine-readable reference rules. It does not determine person-specific eligibility, enrol beneficiaries, manage provider contracts, or adjudicate claims; those functions sit in operational systems outside DPI-H — Beneficiary Registries and enrolment platforms, Contract and Benefits Management Systems (CBMS), provider contracting and tariff systems, prior-authorisation and claims adjudication engines, and public financial management (PFM) systems — which consume the shared registries.
- **Canonical person identity versus scheme enrolment and individual eligibility.** The Client Registry (anchored on foundational digital identity where available) resolves canonical person identity, but does not store scheme membership, premium or contribution status, social-protection exemptions, household contribution groupings, or benefit utilisation. Those records belong to operational beneficiary and enrolment systems (and cross-sector social-protection linkages under data-minimisation safeguards), which reconcile enrolment and claims records against the shared Client Registry identity to support person-specific eligibility determination and cross-scheme duplicate-enrolment checks.
- **Facility and workforce credentials versus provider empanelment and contracting.** The Health Facility Registry and Health Workforce Registry establish authoritative facility and practitioner identity, licensing, service capability, and professional registration status. They do not establish provider empanelment, payer-provider contracts, service authorisation, or claim and payment eligibility; those are operational financing relationships managed in provider-contracting and CBMS platforms that reference authoritative facility and workforce identifiers.
- **Product and terminology references versus pricing, tariffs, and claims adjudication.** The Product Registry and Terminology Service provide standardised identifiers and clinical codes for medicines, commodities, diagnoses, and procedures, but accurate pricing and reimbursement additionally require operational tariff schedules, payer-provider contracts, fee schedules, clinical coding and bundling rules, benefit limits, payment methods (such as capitation, case-based/DRG, or fee-for-service), executable claims logic, and adjudication systems that assess the sufficiency and provenance of service-delivery evidence.
- **Payment decisions and financial controls versus payment settlement rails.** Foundational payment DPI, banking interfaces, and treasury rails initiate, settle, and reconcile monetary transfers, but they do not determine whether a payment is accurate, lawful, contractually compliant, or appropriately authorised. Decisions on benefit coverage, claim validity, provider contract compliance, and financial control sit in operational health-financing, PFM, budget, contract, attendance, and payroll systems, which authorise payments before instructing payment rails to move funds and which govern timely health-worker and provider remuneration beyond DPI-H.
- **Registry linkage versus comprehensive fraud and integrity governance.** Linking claims and enrolment records to authoritative Client, Facility, Workforce, Product, Supplier, and Benefits Package registries supports integrity controls and helps detect duplicate enrolment or phantom entities, but registry linkage does not by itself prevent fraud, corruption, false claims, upcoding, collusion, or leakage. Safeguarding health funds requires combining registry verification with operational clinical and financial audit, anomaly detection, investigation workflows, graduated sanctions, beneficiary and provider appeal mechanisms, internal financial controls, and independent oversight.
