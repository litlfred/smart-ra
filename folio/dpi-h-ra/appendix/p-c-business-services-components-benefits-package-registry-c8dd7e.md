1. The registry is consumed by operational systems that need to know what a benefit package covers when verifying eligibility, authorising care, or adjudicating claims. Operational eligibility services query the registry's published package definitions and combine them with individual enrolment records in the Beneficiary Registry to determine whether a specific service is covered for a given person. Claims management and adjudication systems consume the benefit package definitions and testable rule artefacts held in the registry alongside payer-provider contracts, fee schedules, clinical coding rules, and evidence of service delivery to adjudicate submitted claims. Analytics and reporting systems draw on the registry to produce population-level coverage assessments, national health accounts analyses, and purchasing performance reports. Beneficiary-facing portals, health wallets, and community support channels draw on the registry — paired with plain-language, multilingual, accessible explanations and assisted-access support — to help individuals understand and use their entitlements.
1. Two adjacent operational components are architecturally distinct from the registry and should not be conflated with it.
  1. The Contract and Benefits Management System (CBMS) manages the operational relationships between payers, providers, and beneficiaries — handling enrolment workflows, provider empanelment and payer-provider contracts, negotiated tariffs and fee schedules, premium or contribution collection, person-specific eligibility determination, prior authorisation, claims adjudication, and payment instruction generation. It consumes the Benefits Package Registry as its reference source while managing dynamic, transactional operations that sit outside DPI-H. Any country-specific CBMS implementation should reconcile enrolment and claims records against the shared Client Registry identity and support a standard cross-scheme check for duplicate enrolment, establishing a common interoperability baseline for eligibility verification and integrity controls across schemes.
  1. The Beneficiary Registry records which individuals and households are enrolled in which schemes, under what coverage terms, and for what validity periods; it is an operational scheme-management component that reconciles person identity against the Client Registry and references the package definitions held in the Benefits Package Registry so operational systems can resolve coverage for specific individuals without storing scheme membership inside the Client Registry or the Benefits Package Registry.
1. The registry also supports cross-sectoral coordination with systems outside the health sector. Eligibility for publicly financed or subsidised benefit packages frequently depends on social protection status, household income or means assessment, poverty classification, or employment data held in external registries. For example, in national health–social protection integrations — such as Türkiye's Integrated Social Assistance Information System (ISAIS), which conducts household socioeconomic and income assessments used to determine eligibility for state-subsidised General Health Insurance coverage, or comparable social-registry linkages in other countries — four distinct functions are kept separate: (a) canonical person identification through foundational digital identity and the Client Registry; (b) authoritative publication of benefit packages and general eligibility criteria in the Benefits Package Registry; (c) household income or social-assistance assessment within the social-protection system; and (d) operational beneficiary enrolment, reassessment when household circumstances change, and entitlement administration within the Beneficiary Registry and CBMS. Adapting this pattern across country settings requires attention to transferable lessons and limitations: institutional prerequisites (clear statutory mandates, cross-ministry coordination agreements, and reliable identity linkage), remaining coverage gaps (such as informal-sector mobility, lag times when household income changes, and barriers faced by people without digital access unless assisted-access and plain-language communication channels are provided), and strict data-protection and data-minimisation safeguards that restrict cross-sector exchange to minimal eligibility assertions rather than sharing clinical data with social-protection agencies or raw socioeconomic dossiers with clinical providers. Downstream, foundational digital payment rails (e-payment gateways, treasury and banking interfaces) and operational public financial management (PFM), budget, attendance, and payroll systems execute payment settlement and reconciliation only after operational health-financing and financial-control systems have confirmed that a payment is accurate, lawful, contractually compliant, and appropriately authorised.
