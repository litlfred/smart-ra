- **Scale to context and support offline-first operation.** The architecture should be designed so that the capability required to participate matches the resources of different settings — including rural, remote, and mountainous areas with intermittent connectivity — through offline-first workflows, local caching, asynchronous synchronisation, and low-bandwidth exchange patterns, as well as low-complexity workflows suited to varying levels of digital literacy among health workers and administrative staff.
- **Degrade gracefully and guarantee non-digital routes of equivalent standing.** The absence of a given foundational layer — continuous connectivity, a mature national identity system, personal digital devices, or particular facility infrastructure — should reduce what is automated rather than lock a setting or population out entirely. Every function on which a right, clinical service, or health entitlement depends must maintain an assisted and non-digital route of equivalent standing (such as paper-bridged, facility-mediated, or community-health-worker-assisted pathways) so that participation, care access, and consent never depend on individual smartphone ownership or any single digital channel.
- **Accommodate variation in functional ability, disability, and literacy across the life course.** Countries should require age-inclusive and disability-accessible interaction patterns aligned with international accessibility standards (such as WCAG and ITU-T F.780.2), multimodal channels (including visual, audio, voice, USSD, and tactile formats), plain-language communication, and accessible consent mechanisms — including assisted, delegated, or documented oral consent where legally permitted rather than relying solely on written forms or self-service digital interfaces.
- **Treat language as a clinical data-quality and semantic requirement, not only a presentation concern.** The architecture must not assume that the person seeking care and the health worker share a fluent language — an assumption that routinely fails in multilingual states, rural communities served by clinicians trained in a national or administrative language, and migrant, refugee, or displacement settings. History or consent captured across an unbridged language barrier is systematically less complete and less accurate, and that deficit propagates directly into shared registries and longitudinal records. Supporting local languages, culturally acceptable communication, interpreter or mediation workflows, and the capability to capture history in the person's own language and render it accurately into the working language and governed terminology of the health system should therefore be treated as a data-quality and conformance requirement for systems that write to the shared record.
- **Assess distributional effects, ensure affordability, and guard against discriminatory data practices.** Major architectural, standards, and deployment decisions should be evaluated for their distributional effects — examining who gains access, who bears data-entry and compliance burdens, and who risks exclusion — supported by affordable access channels, representative usability testing across diverse population groups and health workers, disaggregated monitoring for differential outcomes and overlapping disadvantages, and safeguards against discriminatory profiling or unnecessary collection of sensitive data.
