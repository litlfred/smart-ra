Countries should design their DPI-H architecture to serve all populations equitably, including those in remote, rural, mountainous, under-resourced, or marginalised settings. At the level of the architecture, equity and inclusion are structural concerns that encompass not only geographic, connectivity, and institutional constraints, but also user-side capability, socioeconomic, and sociocultural realities across the life course: variation in functional ability and disability (including changes in vision, hearing, dexterity, and cognition across age groups, as recognised in accessibility standards such as ITU-T F.780.2), language, general and health literacy, digital literacy and confidence among both patients and health workers, gender, affordability, cultural acceptability, and protection against discriminatory data practices. Unless these factors are treated as architectural concerns rather than interface afterthoughts, a service may be technically available yet practically unusable, and the populations and settings least well served risk being designed out of the system.
