As a design principle, this means designing the architecture so that consent and delegated or supported authority, where captured, can be represented in a common, standards-based form and carried with the data they govern as that data moves between systems. Whether and how those permissions are enforced rests with the systems that hold and exchange the data and with the legal and governance framework that applies to them. The architecture does not set the legal rules that govern consent or representation — what constitutes valid consent, who is authorised to grant, modify, or withdraw permissions on another person's behalf, or what rights of access apply — which remain matters for each country's national law and policy and vary considerably between settings; countries should also assess whether existing legal and regulatory frameworks adequately support supported and delegated agency and undertake necessary reforms where gaps are identified. However, the architecture should be capable of representing and enforcing supported and delegated access where permitted by law. In doing so, it should explicitly distinguish a relationship record (such as a parent–child, guardian, spousal, or household link in the Client Registry, which may support identity resolution and care coordination but does not by itself confer decision-making or permission authority) from current authority to act on another person's behalf, and distinguish assistance provided under the individual's direction from authority exercised by an authorised representative. Delegated or proxy authority should be modelled as a governed, time-bounded lifecycle rather than inferred from relationships — representing the subject, the representative or supporter, verification of the representative and the legal or governance basis (source of authority), the scope, purpose, data categories, and permitted actions, the effective period, current status, modification, expiry or revocation, and audit provenance. Where a country has consent and delegation infrastructure that operates across sectors, the health sector should consider building on it rather than creating a parallel capability, in keeping with the architecture's approach of leveraging foundational digital public infrastructure where it exists. Cross-sector consent infrastructure remains uncommon, however, and the sensitivity of health data and the governance arrangements around it often give rise to health-specific requirements. Where this is so, a health-sector consent capability may be needed, designed to interoperate with national infrastructure should it later become available. Established references such as the IHE privacy and consent profiles and the HL7 FHIR Consent and RelatedPerson resources offer a starting point for how consent, relationships, and delegated authority can be represented and exchanged.
