**In plain language for clinical, patient, and public-interest readers:** Before entering the architectural terminology of this chapter, the core idea can be stated simply. When a person visits a clinic or hospital, safe, continuous care depends on a few everyday essentials: knowing reliably who the patient is, which nurse, midwife, doctor, pharmacist, or community health worker is caring for them, which facility they are in, what medicines or tests are involved, what the clinical terms recorded in their chart mean, and whether the patient has consented to share their information. Today, those essentials are often locked inside separate programme databases, forcing patients to repeat their history and health workers to copy the same information into multiple registers. **Foundational DPI (DPI-F)** provides the general public utilities a country uses across all sectors — such as national digital identity, digital payments, and secure data exchange. **Digital Public Infrastructure for Health (DPI-H)** adds the shared health-specific building blocks on top of those utilities. DPI-H is organised in **two layers**: (1) a *foundational shared data layer* that keeps trustworthy national directories of patients, health workers, facilities, and health products alongside a person's longitudinal health record and shared clinical vocabularies; and (2) a *business services layer* that uses those directories to support clinical decision support, disease surveillance, routine health reporting, benefit packages, and supply-chain verification. **REDDHI** (*Resilient Essential Data and Digital Health Infrastructure*) is the practical starting core of those shared directories and records that every country works to put in place first, sustained by the **full-STAC** foundation (open **S**tandards, open **T**echnologies, open **A**rchitectures, and open **C**ontent such as WHO SMART Guidelines). **Interoperability** means these systems can exchange information without losing its clinical meaning, and **conformance** means verifying that local clinic or hospital software follows these agreed national rules so patient records stay accurate, private, and usable across the health system.

At the level of detail of a national health system, the reference architecture describes the shared capabilities a national health system requires and the DPI-H components that deliver them. These components build on foundational DPI where it exists and are leveraged by the health programmes and point-of-service applications operating across the health system. What the architecture does not describe is the point-of-service applications and operational systems those programmes use to deliver their specific functions — electronic medical records, order-management platforms, warehouse-management tools, forecasting applications, and disease-specific programme tools. Such applications are important parts of a functioning digital health ecosystem, but they consume shared infrastructure rather than constitute it, and the architecture defines what DPI-H would need to provide so that they can be built, connected, and operated effectively. Anything it describes as a component should therefore be read as shared infrastructure that delivers one or more capabilities, not as a prescription to procure a specific digital system.
