Capacity is not only technical or limited to specialist architecture teams. Introducing shared infrastructure changes how programmes work, how data is captured and shared, and where decisions are made, and these changes are sustained only where the accompanying change management — stakeholder engagement, structured training, workflow redesign, and clear communication of benefit — is planned and resourced as deliberately as the technology itself. In particular, countries should address two widespread implementation barriers explicitly: first, **variability in digital literacy among healthcare professionals and administrative staff** (which affects adoption and data quality in high-, middle-, and low-income settings alike) by defining minimum digital health competencies for frontline roles and providing structured pre-service and in-service training, protected learning time, mentoring, and low-complexity point-of-care workflows; and second, **general illiteracy, low health literacy, and limited digital literacy among patients, alongside linguistic diversity and cultural factors affecting acceptability and trust**, by resourcing multilingual patient navigation, community health worker and interpreter mediation, culturally adapted communication, and assisted and non-digital service pathways of equivalent standing.
