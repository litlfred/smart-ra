- Impact depends on baselines captured at the outset: the current-state assessment in section 4.1 should record not only what exists technically but the starting values of the outcomes the architecture is expected to influence — including baseline gaps in connectivity, health worker and patient digital literacy, language coverage, and populations missing from formal registries — so that change can later be attributed with confidence.
- Alongside outcome measurement, a process and distributional evaluation — how faithfully plans translate into practice, who gains access, who bears data-entry and compliance burdens, who risks exclusion, how well assisted and non-digital routes of equivalent standing perform in practice, and what unintended effects emerge — gives a fuller picture than aggregate outcome indicators alone.
- Where lawful, proportionate, and ethically justified, impact evaluation should disaggregate access, data quality, and outcomes across combinations of overlapping characteristics (such as geography, poverty, disability, functional ability, language, literacy, age, gender, and migration or displacement status), distinguish internet or device access from basic digital skills and health literacy, and explicitly account for populations underrepresented in or absent from formal datasets.
